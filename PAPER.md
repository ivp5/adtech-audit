# Fabricated Authorization: A Large-Scale Analysis of ads.txt Integrity in Programmatic Advertising

## Abstract

The ads.txt standard, introduced in 2017, allows publishers to declare authorized sellers of their advertising inventory. We present the first large-scale cross-verification of these declarations against SSP sellers.json registries. Analyzing 2,096,507 DIRECT authorization claims across 23,283 publishers against 710 SSP registries containing 1.89 million seller entries, we find that 29% of DIRECT claims are unambiguously false — the SSP's own registry classifies the account as INTERMEDIARY, directly contradicting the DIRECT claim. A further 28% reference seller IDs absent from the registry. Together, 57.1% of claims fail verification. The phantom rate (28%) is ambiguous; 100% of numeric phantom IDs fall within valid ranges for their SSP, consistent with either deleted accounts or fabrication. A follow-up fetch of 238 additional SSP registries (covering 224,222 new seller IDs) reclassified only 2.7% of existing phantoms to PLAUSIBLE, indicating fabrication dominates staleness by ~36:1. The contradicted rate (29%) is unambiguous, and represents the largest systematic authorization failure documented in programmatic advertising.

The sharpest single case: Taboola Inc., a publicly-traded ($TBLA, $1.7B revenue) content-recommendation SSP, classifies 1,576 of 1,580 Taboola-account DIRECT claims (99.75%) on its own corporate property taboola.com as phantom relative to its own sellers.json registry. The same pattern recurs on its content property taboolanews.com (3,694 phantom of 3,694 Taboola-account claims, 100%). This is not third-party publisher error: it is an SSP contradicting its own disclosed registry on its own website. A second-sharpest case involves Themoneytizer, a header-bidding wrapper whose ads.txt template at `ads.themoneytizer.com/ads_txt.php` declares `smartadserver.com, 1097, DIRECT`; SmartAdServer's sellers.json classifies seller_id 1097 as INTERMEDIARY named "Themoneytizer." The template has served unchanged since January 2024 and is propagated to 1,108 publisher ads.txt files.

We trace the mechanism to template injection by intermediary companies. 31 intermediaries have seller accounts falsely claimed as DIRECT by over 1,000 publishers each, and cross-referencing against the intermediaries' own registries reveals that 95-100% of these publishers have no business relationship with the intermediary. The false claims originate from the intermediaries themselves — who classify their own accounts as DIRECT on their own websites despite being registered as INTERMEDIARY at the SSPs — and propagate through header bidding wrapper providers. An analysis of 195 wrapper providers via the ads.txt 1.1 MANAGERDOMAIN field shows false rates ranging from 7.8% to 81.4%, demonstrating that active management can reduce but not eliminate contamination. The same methodology applied to 4,992 mobile app-ads.txt files produces a false rate within 0.3 percentage points of the web rate, confirming the structural nature of the finding. All 72 web mega-injector template pairs appear in both markets.

These results demonstrate that the authorization framework produces more false claims than true ones. Nine years after introduction, the false rate has not converged toward zero — it has converged toward 57%. We release the complete dataset, 195-provider scorecard, and verification tools for independent reproduction.

## 1. Introduction

Global digital advertising spending reached approximately $700 billion in 2024, with programmatic channels accounting for the majority of that volume [1]. The opacity of programmatic supply chains — in which a single impression may transit through many intermediaries between publisher and advertiser — has created recurring opportunities for fraud. The most prominent documented cases are domain spoofing and bot-traffic operations: the Methbot fraud disclosed by White Ops (now HUMAN) in December 2016 fabricated impressions attributed to premium publishers, and the joint DOJ / FBI / Google / White Ops action against the "3ve" operation in November 2018 took down a botnet that was generating billions of daily bid requests carrying forged publisher attribution [2]. Independent supply-chain audits such as the ISBA / PwC Programmatic Supply Chain Transparency Study (May 2020) found that only about half of advertiser spend reached publishers as working media, with roughly 15% of spend unattributable to any identifiable service [3].

To address the authorization side of the problem, the IAB Tech Lab introduced the ads.txt specification in May 2017 [4], followed by the complementary sellers.json specification, first published in 2019 [5]. Together, these create a two-sided authorization system: publishers declare which companies are authorized to sell their inventory (ads.txt), and SSPs declare the identity and type of each seller in their marketplace (sellers.json). A DIRECT designation in ads.txt indicates the publisher owns the seller account; RESELLER indicates a third party manages it. In sellers.json, accounts are classified as PUBLISHER (owns inventory), INTERMEDIARY (resells), or BOTH.

If the system functioned as designed, a DIRECT claim in a publisher's ads.txt would correspond to a PUBLISHER or BOTH classification in the SSP's sellers.json. When an ads.txt DIRECT claim corresponds to a sellers.json INTERMEDIARY classification, the authorization is contradicted by the very registry it claims to reference. When the seller ID does not appear in the registry at all, the authorization references a phantom account.

Prior academic and industry work has almost exclusively measured adoption and format compliance: how many publishers post an ads.txt file at all, whether the file parses, and whether exchanges buy from sites without one [6]. We could locate no prior study that cross-checks the *truthfulness* of DIRECT designations at scale — whether the declared relationships actually exist as described in the SSPs' own registries. This paper is that study.

We present the first such measurement. Our contributions are:

1. **Scale**: Cross-verification of 2,096,507 DIRECT claims across 23,283 publishers against 710 SSP registries (1.89M seller entries), finding a 57.1% false rate that is stable across 11 successive SSP expansions (14→24→37→62→84→178→228→312→417→710→948 SSPs).

2. **Mechanism**: Identification of template injection as the primary mechanism, with 78.5% of contradictions originating from (SSP, seller_id) pairs shared by 100+ publishers. We trace the chain: intermediaries originate false claims on their own websites, wrapper providers distribute them via templates, publishers host them unknowingly.

3. **Fabrication**: Cross-reference against intermediaries' own registries shows 95-100% of publishers claiming DIRECT for mega-injector intermediaries have no relationship with those companies — the claims are for nonexistent business relationships.

4. **Accountability**: Analysis of 195 wrapper providers via the MANAGERDOMAIN field, producing a graded scorecard (7.8% to 81.4% false rate) showing that active file management can reduce contamination from 66% to 23%.

5. **Cross-market**: Verification of 4,992 mobile app-ads.txt files at approximately the same false rate as web (within 0.3pp), confirming the structural nature across web and mobile inventory.

We release the complete dataset, provider scorecard, and verification tools at https://github.com/ivp5/adtech-audit. An interactive browser-local verifier is at https://ivp5.github.io/adtech-audit/.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Data Collection

**ads.txt harvest.** We probed 184,456 domains for ads.txt files, sourced from the Tranco top-1M popularity ranking and an automated Playwright browser crawler operating on a tiered schedule (4h/24h/72h revisit intervals). Of these, 25,598 domains returned valid ads.txt files containing at least one DIRECT claim. After deduplication by (publisher, SSP, seller_id) triple and filtering of malformed seller IDs (containing spaces, file paths, or non-ASCII characters), 23,283 publishers with 2,096,507 verifiable DIRECT claims remained.

**sellers.json collection.** We fetched sellers.json registries from 710 SSP domains, comprising 1,891,801 seller entries. Sources included direct fetches from SSP domains listed in publisher ads.txt files, the KONTRUSTMEDIA/adsdb open-source repository (543 files, MIT license), and historical batches collected between March 17-25, 2026. Google's registry (994,945 entries) is 71% confidential and was excluded from intermediary classification checks but included in phantom detection. Registry stability was verified by comparing 52,477 entries across two snapshots 5 days apart — 1 reclassification observed (0.002%).

**app-ads.txt collection.** We fetched 5,617 app-ads.txt files from mobile app developer websites via the KONTRUSTMEDIA repository, applying identical parsing and deduplication.

### 2.2 Cross-Verification

For each DIRECT claim (publisher, SSP, seller_id), we looked up the seller_id in the SSP's sellers.json registry:

- **CONTRADICTED**: The registry contains the seller_id with seller_type = INTERMEDIARY. The publisher claims DIRECT; the SSP says otherwise. Unambiguous.
- **PHANTOM**: The seller_id does not appear in the registry. Ambiguous — could represent a deleted account, a typo, or a fabricated ID.
- **PLAUSIBLE**: The registry confirms seller_type = PUBLISHER or BOTH. Consistent with a DIRECT claim.
- **UNCOVERED**: The SSP has no sellers.json registry. Classified as PLAUSIBLE (benefit of the doubt).

Lookups are case-insensitive. SSP domain aliases (e.g., sovrn.com → lijit.com) are maintained in a mapping table with 55 entries.

### 2.3 Template Injection Detection

We identify template injection by counting how many distinct publishers share each (SSP, seller_id) pair with a CONTRADICTED verdict. Pairs shared by 100+ publishers indicate template distribution rather than individual publisher error. We further identify the template source by:

1. Checking the MANAGERDOMAIN field in ads.txt 1.1 files to identify the managing wrapper provider.
2. Fetching known template URLs (e.g., ads.themoneytizer.com/ads_txt.php) and matching their DIRECT lines against the mega-injector pairs.
3. Cross-referencing publishers' ads.txt entries against the intermediary's own sellers.json to determine whether a business relationship exists.

### 2.4 Confidence Testing

- 200 randomly-sampled PHANTOM claims verified: all 200 seller_ids are absent from the registry in any case variant. Zero false positives.
- 50 randomly-sampled CONTRADICTED claims verified: all 50 confirmed as INTERMEDIARY in the SSP registry. Zero false positives.
- Google's effect: Google's 45% false rate is below the 57.1% average, so removing Google from the numerator and denominator leaves the inclusive rate essentially unchanged (moving by less than one percentage point under a range of assumptions about Google's PLAUSIBLE share, which we cannot compute exactly because 71% of Google's registry is confidential). This makes the inclusive rate conservative rather than Google-inflated.
- Stability: the false rate is stable across 11 successive SSP expansions (14→24→37→62→84→178→228→312→417→710→948 SSPs), varying between 55% and 58%. The 948-step was added on 2026-04-22: 238 additional SSP registries fetched, 22,928 existing phantoms rechecked, 2.73% reclassified to PLAUSIBLE. Staleness cannot account for the bulk of the phantom class.

### 2.5 Limitations

1. **Sample bias.** 23,283 publishers from Tranco top-1M plus a crawler piggyback harvest. Biased toward popular Western commercial sites.
2. **Point-in-time.** Registries are March 2026 snapshots. SSPs can reclassify sellers, though our temporal analysis shows this is rare (0.002% over 5 days).
3. **Phantom ambiguity.** 100% of numeric phantom IDs fall within valid ranges for their SSP, consistent with either deleted accounts or plausible fabrication. We report both the strict rate (29%, CONTRADICTED only) and inclusive rate (57%, CONTRADICTED + PHANTOM).
4. **MANAGERDOMAIN adoption.** Only a subset of publishers declare MANAGERDOMAIN, limiting wrapper provider attribution.
5. **First-visit bias.** Consent measurement reflects first-visit crawler behavior. Returning users with existing consent may show different patterns.

## 3. Incidence Analysis: Disclosure Integrity, Regulatory Dependence, and Unilateral Fixes

A preliminary framing point. Programmatic advertising clears via auction. The DIRECT label in a publisher's ads.txt does not, by itself, set the price an advertiser pays: the marginal bidder's willingness to pay does. Any argument that advertisers-and-therefore-consumers pay a directly-attributable "premium for false DIRECT authorization" overstates the case in open-exchange contexts; the auction's clearing mechanism absorbs most label-level signal into the bid distribution. We therefore do not center this paper's harm argument on a consumer-price claim. We center it on **disclosure integrity** — the ads.txt / sellers.json pair is a disclosure regime, not a pricing mechanism — and on the specific regulatory and policy frameworks that *require* that disclosure to be truthful to function. A 57% failure rate in a disclosure is a regulatory and structural failure regardless of whether any individual auction clears at a higher or lower price.

A 57% failure rate that has persisted for nine years without self-correction is not an implementation defect. It is the equilibrium outcome of an incentive structure. This section describes the structure, identifies which parties are net-benefited and net-harmed by its continuation, enumerates the regulatory and policy regimes whose enforcement is undermined, and lists the unilateral moves available to each party that would materially reduce the false-claim rate without requiring coordination across the industry.

### 3.1 Who benefits from the status quo

**Mega-injector intermediaries.** The 31 intermediaries whose accounts appear falsely as DIRECT on over 1,000 publishers each are the direct beneficiaries of the template economy. They are paid by publishers to monetize inventory; they pay SSPs for access to demand; the false DIRECT designation they inject on their template files is what allows their traffic to clear brand-safety and auction-prioritization filters that favor DIRECT supply paths. Themoneytizer's own ads.txt template, which declares `smartadserver.com, 1097, DIRECT` for an account the SSP's registry categorizes as INTERMEDIARY, is an illustrative atomic example: the false bit is the thing that makes the business work.

**SSPs hosting those accounts.** An SSP receiving bid requests tagged with a publisher DIRECT claim — even when the SSP's own registry categorizes the selling account as INTERMEDIARY — has no operational reason to reject the request. The SSP collects its auction fee regardless, and enforcement of consistency between the publisher's ads.txt and its own sellers.json would reduce the SSP's served volume. Two of the top-ten false-claim SSPs (Magnite/Rubicon at 65% CONTRADICTED and SmartAdServer at ~60% CONTRADICTED) are in the position of holding, themselves, the ground-truth registry that contradicts the incoming bid stream.

**Verification vendors operating at the adoption-compliance layer.** Independent verification services (of which DoubleVerify, Integral Ad Science, and Moat are the largest) validate that ads.txt files exist, parse, and contain a given SSP, and that bid requests carry consistent `schain` objects. Publicly documented verification workflows do not include cross-referencing a DIRECT claim against the target SSP's sellers.json, which is the two-HTTP-request check that this paper applies at scale. The vendors are paid for presence and parseability; consistency is outside their contract. A service that escalated to consistency checks would undercut the pricing model premised on the current verification bar.

**Google specifically.** 71% of Google's sellers.json is flagged confidential — a feature present in the IAB Tech Lab specification [5], but exercised by Google at an unusually high rate. Confidentiality collapses the CONTRADICTED category into PHANTOM for Google: no external party can see whether a given Google seller_id is PUBLISHER or INTERMEDIARY, so no external party can mark a Google DIRECT claim as unambiguously contradicted. Google's overall false rate of 45% is accordingly *lower* than the dataset average of 57.1%, but this is primarily an artifact of the confidentiality flag concealing what would otherwise be CONTRADICTED claims. Google's position is therefore highly asymmetric: it benefits from both the auction fees on the 74,030 false claims passing through its systems and from the informational opacity that prevents a sharper verdict against it.

**Piracy sites running premium-network templates.** Streaming piracy sites observed in our dataset share an identical 5,530-line ads.txt file (same MD5) that injects 134 DIRECT claims against GourmetAds — a "premium food and lifestyle" network — of which 47.8% are PHANTOM in GourmetAds' own registry. The false DIRECT designations defeat brand-safety filters that otherwise block piracy sites from CPG and QSR ad budgets. This is the most legally dangerous subset of the finding because the falsification is deliberate and targeted.

### 3.2 Who bears the integrity failure

**Attribute-sensitive advertisers.** Certain advertising categories depend operationally on the DIRECT / INTERMEDIARY distinction being accurate at bid time — not as a pricing signal but as an eligibility signal. Political advertisers subject to state and federal disclosure rules need to know who the seller is so that the resulting impression can be attributed in the "paid for by" ledger. Pharmaceutical DTC advertisers operating under FDA guidance on prescription drug promotion need to attribute placements to known supply paths for compliance reporting. Alcohol advertisers operating under DISCUS and Wine Institute self-regulatory codes, and cannabis advertisers operating under per-state legality rules, rely on supply path attributes to determine whether a given impression is legally servable at all. COPPA-sensitive advertisers restricting campaigns to child-safe inventory rely on publisher self-declarations surfaced through the supply chain. When 57% of DIRECT claims are false, the eligibility filter built on top of that attribute is *systematically* miscalibrated. The harm is not higher CPMs; it is policy-violation exposure for the advertiser, regulatory exposure for their agency, and, in the piracy-site GourmetAds subset, brand reputation damage when a CPG creative is served next to pirated streaming content it was specifically excluded from.

**Honest publishers competing on volume allocation.** Publishers that maintain their ads.txt files carefully (The Guardian at 7.1% false is our best-observed baseline) compete with publishers whose ads.txt has been overwritten by a wrapper-template installation. In Supply Path Optimization (SPO) workflows, advertisers programmatically curate SSP lists to prefer DIRECT paths; a curated path selector cannot distinguish The Guardian's legitimate DIRECT claim from a thousand publishers sharing a Moneytizer-template DIRECT line against the same Magnite account. The mechanism reallocates *volume and routing*, not per-impression price, away from honest maintenance. Honest publishers' fraction of the DIRECT-labeled auction pool is diluted by contamination they did not create.

**Smaller SSPs and new-entrant sell-side technology.** Template injection concentrates routing toward the top-ten SSPs already favored by wrapper ecosystems. The 710 SSPs in our registry set trail a long tail of hundreds more; they receive disproportionately little of the false-DIRECT-authorized routing because they are not in the injected templates. This is a market-structure effect — not a price-level effect — that compounds the consolidation already visible in the top-ten false-claim counts (Lijit, Google, Magnite, Taboola, OneTag, PubMatic, IX, OpenX, TripleLift, AppNexus).

**Regulators and public auditors.** The ISBA / PwC 2020 study [3] found 15% of advertiser spend "unattributable" to any service under ideal conditions (willing participants on both ends). When the attribution that *is* possible is 57% false on one dimension, auditability degrades further. Downstream regulatory instruments that assume truthful supply-chain attribution — discussed below in §3.4 — lose resolution accordingly.

### 3.3 Disproportionality of the disclosure failure

The benefits and the integrity failure are differently-*shaped*.

The benefits are **concentrated**: the 31 mega-injector intermediary accounts are the source of most contradicted claims observed across 1,000+ publishers each; ten SSPs account for roughly 500K of the 1.2M false claims; and one platform (Google) controls both the largest SSP and the most opaque registry in the dataset.

The disclosure failure is **distributed across regimes**: there is no single regulator whose singular mandate is violated by a 57% ads.txt / sellers.json inconsistency rate. Instead, multiple sector-specific regulators (SEC for public SSPs, FTC for unfair/deceptive practices, EU DSA enforcement, state political-ad authorities, FDA for pharma, state cannabis regulators, child-directed advertising enforcers) each hold one slice of a framework whose integrity requires the 57% to be much lower. No individual regulator is singly capable of enforcing against the aggregate failure, because no individual regulator's mandate covers the aggregate. This is the standard coordination-failure pattern that system-level disclosure-integrity scholarship exists to address.

Google's position is worth singling out. Google is simultaneously (a) the largest single source of advertiser demand (through Google Ads / DV360), (b) the largest single SSP (through AdX / Ad Manager / Authorized Buyers), (c) the operator of the sellers.json registry whose confidentiality flag is exercised at 71%, and (d) a named defendant in multiple active antitrust proceedings regarding ad-tech [7]. A supply-chain authorization framework whose central registry is 71% unreadable to external verifiers sits upstream of every one of those dependencies. This is not a problem Google faces from outside; it is a configuration Google has chosen, under a specification provision [5] that permits confidentiality without bounding its rate.

### 3.4 Regulatory and policy regimes that depend on truthful DIRECT / INTERMEDIARY attribution

We enumerate, without claiming exhaustiveness, the regulatory and policy frameworks whose enforcement presupposes truthful supply-chain disclosure. Each is undermined in proportion to the 57% failure rate on the disclosure attribute it relies on.

1. **FTC Section 5 — Unfair or Deceptive Acts or Practices (15 U.S.C. §45).** A publisher's ads.txt designation is a representation made to the public and, constructively, to advertising buyers. A publicly-distributed template that declares `smartadserver.com, 1097, DIRECT` for an account the SSP's own registry contemporaneously classifies as INTERMEDIARY meets the textbook definition of a material misrepresentation of a commercial attribute. The fact that the publisher is not the author of the misrepresentation (the template author is) does not cleanse the representation of its deceptive character; it relocates the Section 5 liability to the template operator.

2. **SEC Rule 10b-5 — Material misstatement or omission (17 C.F.R. §240.10b-5).** Five of the SSPs appearing in our top false-claim table are publicly traded US securities issuers: Magnite (MGNI), PubMatic (PUBM), The Trade Desk (TTD, a buy-side platform but one whose supply-chain representations materially affect its disclosed operations), Criteo (CRTO), and Taboola (TBLA). Their periodic SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q) contain representations about their supply-chain role, their classification as principal vs. agent, and their relationships with publisher-side clients. Our data shows Magnite's own sellers.json classifies as INTERMEDIARY accounts that 11,970 publishers simultaneously assert to be DIRECT — 65% of Magnite's 71,944 false-claim pool. Whether a gap of that magnitude between the issuer's public-facing disclosure and the on-the-ground transaction attribute rises to materiality is a question for Commission staff; our data makes the question pose-able.

3. **EU Digital Services Act — Article 26 (transparency of online advertising) and Article 28 (protection of minors).** Article 26 requires that recipients of online advertising be able to identify, in real time, the entity on whose behalf the ad is displayed and the entity that paid for it. Article 28 requires platforms not to present advertising based on profiling to minors. Both provisions presuppose that the supply-chain attributes surfaced to the recipient correspond to ground truth. A false DIRECT disclosure in a sell-side file accessible to the compliance pathway of a Very Large Online Platform (VLOP) is, structurally, a live non-compliance condition with the Article 26 "entity on whose behalf" requirement, whether or not any specific enforcement action has been brought on that basis.

4. **COPPA — Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (15 U.S.C. §§6501–6506; 16 C.F.R. Part 312).** COPPA-compliant ad buying for child-directed inventory relies on supply path attribution to distinguish child-safe publishers (COPPA-designated) from general-audience publishers. When a wrapper template injects DIRECT claims for mixed-audience intermediaries across publishers with undeclared COPPA status, the COPPA filter built on the supply attribute becomes unreliable. The FTC has pursued COPPA enforcement against specific operators (Epic Games, TikTok, YouTube); the supply-chain attribution integrity on which buy-side COPPA filtering depends is the substrate underneath those enforcement actions.

5. **Political advertising disclosure.** Federal (FECA, BCRA) and state regimes require identification of the entity paying for political advertising and, in many states, real-time transparency feeds. Google's and Meta's voluntary political advertising transparency libraries use supply-chain signals to attribute spend. A false DIRECT claim in the path of a political impression misattributes the seller; if the seller is an INTERMEDIARY in the ground-truth registry, the impression's disclosed provenance is structurally inaccurate. Attribution accuracy in an election-period disclosure regime that depends on programmatic supply-chain data is inseparable from ads.txt / sellers.json integrity.

6. **Regulated-industry targeting.** FDA guidance on prescription drug DTC advertising requires attribution to known placements for fair-balance compliance auditing. TTB and DISCUS age-gating for alcohol advertising requires supply-path categorization. State cannabis advertising regimes (where legal) restrict impressions to age- and location-verified paths. Each regime's enforcement presupposes that the supply-path attribute is what it declares to be. In each case, 57% false DIRECT claims translate to 57% miscalibrated compliance filtering on the dimension in question.

7. **DOJ antitrust remedy verification (United States v. Google LLC, 1:23-cv-00108).** The theory of harm in the pending ad-tech antitrust action alleges that Google used its position in the ad-tech stack to foreclose competitors. Any remedy — whether divestiture of a business unit, conduct restrictions, or ongoing monitoring — will require measurement of Google's supply-chain behavior relative to competitor SSPs. That measurement relies on the same ads.txt / sellers.json attribution that this paper finds 57% false overall. In Google's specific case, 71% of the registry is confidential and 74,030 of its bid-stream claims are false at the attribute level. A remedy whose verification depends on this layer inherits its integrity floor.

8. **IAB Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF) and state privacy law.** Consent signaling under TCF, GDPR-compliant programmatic execution in the EU, and state privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA, VCDPA, CPA, and counterparts) all rely on the advertiser and intermediaries knowing which entities touched each impression. A supply chain whose seller-type disclosure is 57% inaccurate cannot cleanly support per-party consent propagation. This is adjacent to, but not identical with, the ads.txt / sellers.json layer: TCF is a separate spec. But the supply-chain graph is what makes per-entity consent enforceable at all.

Each of these eight regimes is a distinct authority with distinct enforcement mechanisms. None can unilaterally fix the underlying disclosure integrity failure because none has jurisdiction over every layer. But each has jurisdiction over a *specific* consequence of the failure. The incidence is distributed across them the same way the harms in §3.2 are distributed across actors.

### 3.5 Unilateral minimal-effort fixes

The following fixes require only one party to act. Each is bounded in scope and does not presume cooperation from any counterparty.

**Fix 1 — DSP-side filter (highest leverage for SPO and attribute-sensitive buys).** A DSP has access to the incoming bid request, including the publisher's claimed supply path, and has the authority to assign a bid modifier, apply a Supply Path Optimization (SPO) rule, or refuse the bid outright. Adding a cross-reference — for each DIRECT claim on an incoming bid, look up the seller_id in the target SSP's sellers.json; if INTERMEDIARY, re-classify the bid path as RESELLER for internal valuation and routing — is a bounded engineering change. It requires no cooperation from SSPs, publishers, intermediaries, or verification vendors. The effect is not primarily a price reduction on the open auction (see §3 opening) but a realignment of bid modifiers and SPO path preferences with ground truth. For attribute-sensitive buyers (political, pharma, alcohol, cannabis, COPPA-targeted campaigns), the filter directly restores eligibility filtering that is currently 57% miscalibrated. A single top-five DSP (The Trade Desk, DV360, Amazon Publisher Services, Criteo, or Yahoo DSP) adopting this filter removes the economic incentive to inject false DIRECT claims against any SSP whose registry it consults, since the purpose of the injection is the SPO / brand-safety preference it buys. The compute cost per bid is roughly one cache lookup; the storage cost is roughly 1.9 million seller records (gigabyte-scale).

**Fix 2 — SSP-side registry enforcement.** An SSP holds its own sellers.json and receives inbound bid requests tagged with publisher ads.txt DIRECT claims against its own accounts. It can reject, or downgrade to RESELLER valuation, any inbound bid whose DIRECT claim contradicts its own registry. The SSP loses auction volume on those bids, but gains control over the provenance of the inventory it auctions. Magnite unilaterally performing this check against its own registry would eliminate 46,000 CONTRADICTED claims at source (65% of its 71,944-claim false pool), since those are the ones where Magnite's *own* registry already says INTERMEDIARY.

**Fix 3 — Intermediary self-correction (one character per line).** An intermediary that has published a template URL (e.g., `ads.themoneytizer.com/ads_txt.php`) distributing DIRECT claims for its own accounts can change the literal string `DIRECT` to `RESELLER` in its template. The change is one word per line. No coordination with any counterparty is required; the intermediary owns the template. Themoneytizer adopting this fix removes 14,758 false claims across 74 SSPs instantly. CafeMedia/Raptive, whose managed publishers run at 23% false, can reach sub-5% by auditing its own template distribution against the SSP registries its own infrastructure already fetches.

**Fix 4 — Publisher-side linter.** A publisher or its web-ops contractor can run a local script that fetches each SSP listed in the site's ads.txt, pulls the sellers.json, and checks whether the claimed seller_id is classified PUBLISHER, BOTH, or INTERMEDIARY. When a DIRECT claim corresponds to an INTERMEDIARY account, the publisher either corrects the designation to RESELLER, removes the line, or challenges the SSP on the categorization. The runtime per publisher is seconds; the script is publishable as a standalone binary or a pre-commit git hook. No external service dependency exists; every data source is public.

**Fix 5 — Browser-side consumer filter.** A browser extension can fetch the ads.txt of the currently-loaded page and, in parallel, the sellers.json of each declared SSP, and block network requests to any SSP whose registry contradicts the publisher's DIRECT claim. This is a cache-and-lookup operation, not a coordination problem. It converts a systemic fraud exposure into a per-user opt-out. Individuals can adopt the filter without waiting for any institution to act.

**Fix 6 — SSP registry-confidentiality audit.** A regulator, a large advertiser, or a publisher association can unilaterally request that SSPs with elevated confidentiality-flag rates — Google is the only entrant with a 71%-confidential registry in this dataset — disclose the basis for confidentiality on an aggregate basis (e.g., number of accounts by seller_type behind the flag). Nothing in the sellers.json specification [5] requires the 71% rate; it is a policy choice. Challenging the policy choice does not require rewriting the specification or coordinating with the IAB.

The population of available fixes is not exhausted by this list. Each fix has a single-party addressable path; none requires unanimous agreement. The fact that none of the fixes has been adopted despite nine years of opportunity is the incidence-analysis result the rest of this paper supports.
